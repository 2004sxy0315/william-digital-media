WORKSHOP01WEBSITE DEVELOPMENT

Building a space of my own.

Turning a first HTML page into a personal website—and learning how its structure, appearance and publication fit together.

What I did

I built a personal website using HTML and CSS in Visual Studio Code and published it through GitHub Pages. I connected pages through navigation links and added images and workshop reflections. In later revisions, I developed a darker visual style with orange accents and a portrait to make the website feel more personal.

What challenged me

A page working locally did not always mean it worked online. Some images failed to load, and the published website sometimes displayed an older version. These problems helped me distinguish between saving files, uploading them, deploying the website and refreshing the browser.

What I learned

HTML gives content its structure, while CSS controls its presentation. I also began to understand how class connects elements to styling rules and how href connects pages. Publishing made file organisation important: images and stylesheets need to be uploaded with the correct paths. A navigation link also needs an actual destination page.

What comes next

I want to test the website more systematically across screen sizes and check every navigation link and image after publishing. I also want to make the reflections easier to follow, so the website communicates my learning as clearly as it presents my work.

WORKSHOP02WEB SCRAPING

Looking beyond the webpage.

Learning to turn programme listings into data—and to question what the extracted results actually represent.

What I did

I used the workshop’s Python notebook in Google Colab to collect BBC iPlayer programme titles and descriptions with Requests and BeautifulSoup. I exported the results as a CSV file and inspected them in a spreadsheet. I then explored how to adapt the process to ITV by inspecting its programme cards.

What challenged me

I initially expected that changing the website address would be enough. However, ITV organised its information differently. I first selected a decorative background element before locating the relevant programme card, where descriptive information was stored in an aria-label.

What I learned

Scraping depends on understanding a website’s structure, rather than simply copying visible text. My BBC export contained 106 records but 89 distinct titles, showing that a row does not necessarily represent a unique programme. Repeated entries may reflect placement across different sections. A successful export also does not prove that every relevant item was captured.

What comes next

I need to test the adapted ITV extraction against the visible page and check for missing or repeated entries. Recording the collection date, page scope and selection rules would make the dataset easier to interpret and the process easier to repeat.

WORKSHOP03COLLABORATIVE DATA COLLECTION

From a broad question to usable evidence.

Exploring how students describe creative control when working with generative AI.

What I contributed

Our group investigated how students describe their sense of control over creative decisions when using generative AI. My contributions were helping to define the research question, finding relevant sources and organising the evidence into a spreadsheet.

The final independent collection used four studies and contained 14 evidence records: 12 directly relevant records and two background records. The dataset retained source information and coding explanations so that each selection could be traced back to its context. AI assistance supported the collection and initial coding process.

What challenged me

Finding material about AI was easier than finding evidence that answered our specific question. Statements about efficiency, originality or ownership did not necessarily explain students’ control over creative decisions. Organising the spreadsheet required distinguishing direct quotations, researchers’ summaries and our interpretations, rather than treating them as equivalent evidence.

What I learned

Defining the question and collecting the data were connected tasks. A clearer question helped me decide what belonged in the spreadsheet and what should remain background material.

I also recognised that collecting data involves power: published studies already select which experiences to report, and our selection and coding further shape which voices become visible. The 14 records were pieces of evidence, not 14 individual students. Their distribution cannot represent all students or prove that AI generally reduces creative control.

What comes next

I would compare the initial coding with my group members’ interpretations and return to the original passages where we disagree. With more time, I would also seek evidence from a wider range of creative tasks and student backgrounds, while keeping the selection criteria consistent.

WORKSHOP04DATA VISUALISATION

Making patterns visible without losing context.

Using Excel to explore how the presentation of data shapes its interpretation.

What I did

I used Excel to organise data and create different tables during the data visualisation workshop. Building on the previous group task, I considered how structured information could help an audience compare categories and understand the evidence behind our research question.

What I reflected on

The workshop prompted me to think about what an audience needs to understand, rather than simply how to make a presentation look attractive. A table can preserve detail, while a chart can make comparisons easier to notice. Choosing between them depends on the question and the information being presented.

For our dataset, the distinction between evidence records and individual students was especially important. A clear-looking display could still mislead viewers if that distinction was missing.

What I learned

Preparing a visualisation begins with understanding the dataset: what each row represents, what the variables mean and which comparisons are appropriate. Numerical category codes are labels, so calculating their average would not produce a meaningful measure of creative control.

I also learned to consider how titles, labels, colour and category choices guide attention. Presenting data responsibly means making its scope and uncertainty visible, including records whose meaning remains unclear.

What comes next

I want to develop the Excel tables into a small selection of clearly labelled charts, each addressing a specific question. For the creative-control project, I would state that the figures describe selected evidence records and include the sources and limitations. I would then ask someone outside the group to explain what they think the visualisation shows, to check whether the intended message is clear.

WORKSHOP 05 IDENTITY, CLASSIFICATION & DATA

Can a category describe a person?

What I did

I used Sumpter’s Matrix to classify 15 posts from each of 32 Bilibili accounts I follow. Each post was assigned to one of 13 predefined categories.

What I noticed

Deciding whether a post fitted a category required interpretation, especially when its content could overlap several categories. Most selected accounts posted within a narrow range: 15 appeared in one category, 14 in two and three in three.

What I learned

These patterns describe the sampled posts, not the complete identities of the people behind them. They also reflect whom I choose to follow. Classification makes content easier to compare, but fixed categories can hide context and complexity.

What I would improve

I would record brief reasons for ambiguous choices and apply consistent coding rules. Comparing a small sample with another person’s classifications could reveal how our judgements shape the results.
